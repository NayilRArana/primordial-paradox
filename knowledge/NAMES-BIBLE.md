## 3. Candidates in detail

Format: name, what it means, source, plausible for, collision check. "Judgement" marks my call, not a source.

### 3.1 Hero (player, 19, first year, called "a stowaway with a god" by Percy)
The data names him Aeson and no myth figure is assigned to him anywhere in the project. That is the real gap:
he is the one character whose myth we cannot point at. Names below work whichever figure Nayil picks, and
Option A is chosen because Percy's line is literally about carrying a god.

- **Kit** (from Christopher). Late Greek Christophoros, "bearing Christ", from Christos plus phero, "to bear, to
  carry". The legend has Saint Christopher carry the child across a river; he is the patron saint of travellers,
  which is what the hero is. Source: behindthename.com/name/christopher. Plausible for a British 19-year-old;
  short forms read young and unpretentious (judgement). Collision: Christopher Robin is a famous fictional
  bearer of the long form; "Kit" itself is worn by actors and a playwright, no dominant fictional owner.
- Rory. Anglicised Ruaidhri, Old Irish "red king" (ruad "red" plus ri "king"), the last high king of Ireland.
  Source: behindthename.com/name/ruaidhri10. Plausible and common in England and Wales for his birth year
  (judgement). Collision: Rory Gilmore is a famous television character; the name is far too common for one
  bearer to own it, but it is a real risk if Nayil dislikes the association.
- Aeson (keep what the data already says). Greek Aison, of unknown meaning; in myth the father of Jason.
  Source: behindthename.com/name/aeson. Public domain myth name, allowed by PLAN.md line 13. Weakness: it reads
  as a misspelling of Jason or Ethan, and it assigns a father's name to a 19-year-old.

### 3.2 Prometheus (fire, forethought, the interruption and the mercy prison scene)
He is the oldest voice in the game: a Titan, chained to a rock in the myth, and the one who stole fire.

- **Aidan**. Anglicised Aodhan, Old Irish Aedan, "little fire", a diminutive of Aed. Sixth-century king of Dal
  Riata, and the name of early Irish saints. Source: behindthename.com/name/aodha10n. Perfect meaning for the
  fire-bringer; very plausible for a British man born in the 1980s or 1990s (judgement). Collision: many
  bearers, no dominant fictional one.
- Phoenix. The immortal bird that is consumed by fire and rises from its own ashes; the Greek word phoinix means
  "dark red". Source: behindthename.com/name/phoenix. Gives the P letter the Skye rule uses, and the fire
  imagery is exact. Plausible but rarer for a man in his 40s (judgement); reads slightly dramatic, which suits
  a Titan who announces himself.
- Piers. Medieval form of Peter, from the root petra, "rock"; the name of the main character of Piers Plowman.
  Source: behindthename.com/name/piers. The rock is the myth detail most people forget: Prometheus is chained to
  one. Plausible British, P letter, and under used. Collision: Piers Morgan is a real broadcaster, not a
  fictional character.

### 3.3 Atlas (genocide prison scene; the Titan who holds up the sky)
- **Ethan**. Hebrew etan, "solid, enduring, firm". Source: behindthename.com/name/ethan. Endurance is exactly
  what Atlas is punished with. Very plausible for a British man of any age under 45 (judgement). Collision:
  Ethan Hunt and Ethan Frome exist; the name is common enough that neither owns it.
- Stanley. Old English stan, "stone", plus leah, "woodland, clearing", so "stone clearing". Source:
  behindthename.com/name/stanley. Stone is the myth's own ending for Atlas (the mountains carry his name in the
  same entry). Plausible, a little older and plainer, which fits a Titan doing forced labour (judgement).
  Collision: Stanley Kowalski is a famous stage character; the name is common in Britain.
- Atlas. Possibly "enduring", from Greek tlao, "to endure"; the Titan forced to support the heavens. Source:
  behindthename.com/name/atlas. PLAN.md line 13 allows myth names outright. Weakness: Atlas is now a very loud
  product and company name (maps, rockets, databases), so it dates the game.

### 3.4 Ouranos (post credits; the heavens, the first king, the father of the Titans)
He is a mystery scene, so a name with an echo is better than a plain one.
- **Urien**. Old Welsh Urbgen, possibly from the Celtic root orbo, "heir", plus gen, "born of"; a sixth-century
  king of Rheged, later the king of Gore in Arthurian tales. Source: behindthename.com/name/urien. Sounds like
  Ouranos (Ur against Our), is authentically British (Welsh and northern), and is rare enough to feel like a
  secret. Plausible for an older man; rare for anyone young (judgement).
- Owen. Anglicised Owain, with the roots eu and genes, so "well born"; used in Welsh and English. Source:
  behindthename.com/name/owen-1. The O letter, the "well born" meaning (Ouranos is the first king), and utterly
  plausible in Britain at any age. Collision: many fictional bearers, which is the point: none owns it.
- Orion. Meaning uncertain; possibly Greek horion, "boundary, limit", or Akkadian Uru-anna, "light of the
  heavens"; a constellation named for the hunter. Source: behindthename.com/name/orion. "Light of the heavens"
  and a sky figure for the sky god, and the sound echoes Ouranos. Plausible but stylish; check Nayil likes it.

### 3.5 Navy Sailor (tutorial fight, the one who says "You are not on the list")
The sailor is a working man in his twenties serving under Percy, so the name should be ordinary and British.
- Morgan. Old Welsh Morcant or Morgant, possibly Welsh mor, "sea", plus cant, "circle". Source:
  behindthename.com/name/morgan-1. The sea in the name, and the name is normal in a British crew. Collision:
  Morgan le Fay is myth (public domain), not a modern fictional owner; the name is now used for girls more than
  boys in the US, still masculine in Wales (judgement).
- **Dylan**. Welsh dy, "to, toward", plus llanw, "tide, flow"; in the Fourth Branch of the Mabinogi, Dylan son of
  Arianrhod swam like a fish, and the figure may come from an earlier Celtic sea god. Source:
  behindthename.com/name/dylan. The best sea name on the list, and completely plausible for a British man born
  in the 1990s. Collision: the poet and the musician are real people, not fictional owners.
- Piran. Possibly from Ciaran; a fifth-century Irish monk who founded a monastery in Cornwall, patron saint of
  Cornwall. Source: behindthename.com/name/piran. Gives the P letter and the sea county; a Cornish sailor in the
  Navy is a normal British story. Plausible, a little unusual (judgement).

### 3.6 High Street NPC (the one line that changes with the route)
This NPC is the game's quiet judge: it reacts to kills. A herald, or a prophet nobody believes, is the right
myth echo.
- Gabriel. Hebrew Gavriel, "God is my strong man", from gever, "strong man, hero", plus el, "God"; the
  archangel who carries messages. Source: behindthename.com/name/gabriel. A messenger for a route-reactive
  line, and completely plausible in modern Britain. Collision: an archangel is public domain; no modern
  fictional owner.
- **Cass** (from Cassandra). Greek Kassandra, possibly from kekasmai, "to excel, to surpass", plus aner, "man";
  the Trojan princess given prophecy by Apollo and cursed so that nobody would believe her. Source:
  behindthename.com/name/cassandra. The best fit in the whole file: an NPC whose warnings change with your
  kills, and whom the player will not believe. Plausible in Britain for a woman under 40 (judgement).
- Nye. A Welsh diminutive of Aneurin, whose root element is "honor". Source: behindthename.com/name/aneurin.
  Short, British, slightly plain, good for a shopkeeper or a busker, and the "honour" root is the joke when the
  line turns on what you did. Collision: Aneurin Bevan is real, not fictional.

### 3.7 Titan employees (six generic fights in One North Quay and the data centre)
No dialogue, so the cheap and correct answer is a naming scheme, not six biographies. Pick one:
- **Myth names on lanyards** (recommended). Each employee is "H. Coeus", "M. Theia", "I. Hyperion" and so on:
  first initial plus the Titan name, which reads as a company that named its staff after a corporate value set.
  Source: PLAN.md line 13 allows public domain myth names; Theoi (theoi.com) identifies the Titans.
- Modern echoes: give each of the six a modern British first name whose meaning points at its Titan (Fire,
  Memory, Light, Sight, Order, Time), same method as section 3. This needs a second card; it is six more
  sourced names.
- Job titles: THE AUDITOR, THE GRIPPER, THE LEDGER, THE WATCH. Zero naming risk, and it fits a data centre run
  by a CEO who never sees sunlight (judgement).

## 4. Places and entities

Rule used: real places keep their real name only where the game is not claiming to reproduce them exactly
(Nayil already does this with "Faux Wessex Lane Halls"). Everything invented follows a real British naming
pattern so it does not read as fantasy.

### 4.1 The ship (vertical slice)
Royal Navy patrol vessels are named after rivers: Tyne, Severn, Mersey, Clyde, Forth, Medway, Trent, Tamar,
Spey (River-class patrol vessel, Wikipedia, read 2026-10-21). Older RN ships also carry myth and nymph names,
so both routes are in character.
- **HMS Thetis**. Thetis is a Nereid, possibly from Greek thetes, "one who places", from tithemi, "to set, to
  place"; the mother of Achilles. Source: behindthename.com/name/thetis. Sea nymph for a sea ship, and a name
  the Navy has used; short enough for a bulkhead stencil.
- HMS Nereid. The Nereids are the sea nymphs Thetis belongs to, so this is the plainer version of the same idea,
  and it reads well on a grey hull. Source: the same Thetis entry.
- HMS Itchen. A River-class style name; the Itchen is the river at Winchester and Southampton, which grounds the
  ship in the game's own map. Source: River-class naming above. To check on OpenStreetMap before use (the plan
  allows OSM and not Google).

### 4.2 The data centre (map 3, Hades)
- **Coldharbour**. A "cold harbour" was an uninhabited shelter for travellers: a roof, a door, no staff, no food,
  no warmth; from Old English cald plus herbergh, traced by J. C. Hahn (1865) to German kalte Herberge and
  accepted by Merriam-Webster. Source: Wikipedia, Coldharbour Lane, read 2026-10-21. It is also a real London
  street name, which makes "Coldharbour Data Campus" sound like a place that exists.
- Plouton Gate. Hades is Plouton, the god of the hidden wealth of the earth, "from the fertile soil ... to the
  mined wealth of gold, silver and other metals". Source: theoi.com, Haides, read 2026-10-21. Servers as buried
  wealth, which is exactly what a data centre sells.
- The Seam. A coal seam: buried, dark, worked by people who never see daylight. This is coinage, not etymology,
  and it is the plainest of the three.

### 4.3 The London prison
Real London prisons are named after their locality: Belmarsh, Brixton, Feltham, Isis, Pentonville, Thameside,
Wandsworth, Wormwood Scrubs (public prison contact lists, read 2026-10-21). Invented names must therefore sound
like a London district, not like a fantasy keep.
- **HMP Saltmarsh**. Marshland by the Thames, echoing Belmarsh; the dead rising in London come out of wet ground,
  which is the game's own image.
- HMP Asphodel. The asphodel meadows are the dead's fields in the underworld; a Victorian prison with a Greek
  revival name is a very British thing.
- HMP Fenmoor. Plain, flat, eastern: the kind of name a real prison has. Coinage.

### 4.4 The final room (Kronos, AI OS room)
The operating system is the joke: any name ending in OS works.
- **OURANOS**. Greek ouranos, "the heavens", the husband of Gaia and the father of the Titans. Source:
  behindthename.com/name/uranus. The sky as software, and the post credits scene is the same figure, so the
  ending rhymes.
- EREBOS. Erebos is the primordial darkness the underworld sits in; the pun is free.
- CHRONOS. Time, which is Kronos's domain; the pun is the whole thesis of the game.

### 4.5 One North Quay Research Facility
This is a real building: One North Quay, North Quay, Canary Wharf, London E14, a 23-storey life sciences lab
tower developed by Canary Wharf Group with Kadans, under construction when read (2026-10-21; pages: nla.london,
opendoors.construction, programme.openhouse.org.uk). Options: keep the name (it is already public and the game
does not claim accuracy), or "Faux North Quay" to follow the halls convention, or "North Quay Labs" to soften
it. Recommendation: keep, and add the same one-line "a fictional version" note the halls get.
